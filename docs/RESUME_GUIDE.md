# Resume guide

What the builder, its suggestions and the agent are based on. A summary of advice that career centers and hiring
guides agree on, in our own words, with sources at the end. Advice from companies that sell resume tools is
marked as such: their numbers are not independent.

## 1. Structure

- **One column, one page** for students and juniors; two pages only with real experience to fill them.
- **Standard section names** (Summary, Skills, Work Experience, Projects, Education), so both people and
  applicant-tracking systems (ATS) find things.
- **Order by what proves you best.** Experience first if you have relevant jobs; education and projects first if
  you're a student or graduate; skills and projects first if you're changing careers. That's what the templates do.
- **Reverse chronological** inside each section: newest first.
- **Contact line:** city (not the full address), phone, email, LinkedIn, and GitHub or a portfolio if it shows your work.

## 2. Format that machines can read

- Plain text in a simple layout: no text boxes, tables, columns, icons for skills, or text inside images.
- A common font (Calibri, Arial, Georgia), 10–11 pt for text.
- Send a **PDF** made from the Word file unless the form asks for `.docx`. It keeps the layout as you made it.
  Keep it small (under 2 MB); the agent's PDFs are usually 0.1–0.3 MB.
- Country norms differ: in the US, UK and Canada leave out a photo, age and marital status; in some European
  countries a photo is common. When in doubt, leave it out.

## 3. Bullet points

- **Formula:** action verb + what you did + the result. *“Built a Power BI dashboard used by 30 managers, replacing
  a weekly manual report.”*
- **Start with a strong verb** (built, automated, reduced, led, launched). Avoid “responsible for”, “helped”,
  “worked on”: they describe a duty, not an achievement.
- **Numbers** make it concrete: how many, how much, how often, how fast. If you don't know an exact number, a
  careful estimate (“~20 users”) is better than none; never invent one.
- **One or two lines each**, 2–4 bullets per job, more for the most relevant one.

## 4. Summary and skills

- **Summary:** two or three lines: who you are, your main skills, what you're looking for. Mention your degree
  early if it's your main asset.
- **Skills:** grouped lines (`Languages: …`, `Cloud: …`), only things you could discuss in an interview. This is the
  part the agent reorders for each job.

## 5. Tailoring to each job

- Read the posting and note the skills, tools and titles it repeats. Those are the keywords people and ATS look for.
- Use the posting's wording where it's true for you (e.g. “CI/CD” if they write CI/CD), in the skills line and in a
  bullet that shows it.
- Put what matters for this job first: the agent does this by reordering your skills, and never adds a skill you
  don't have.
- Don't stuff keywords or copy the posting: a person reads it after the machine.

## 6. Ghost jobs

A *ghost job* is a real company's posting with no real intention to hire soon (to look like it's growing, to
collect resumes, or a role already filled). It is not a scam. Signs:

- the same job is posted again and again, or stays up for months;
- generic text with no team, manager or start date;
- not listed on the company's own careers page;
- no reply at all, or a process that stops without explanation.

The agent notices reposted jobs and asks you whether to mark them as fake; marked jobs are never offered again.

## 7. Before sending

- Spelling, dates and links checked; file name like `Jane Doe resume.pdf`.
- The *Check my resume* button in the builder flags weak openings, missing numbers, long bullets and common
  keywords for your field that you haven't mentioned (add them only if true).
- *Copy prompt* gives you a ready prompt for Claude to improve the wording without inventing facts.
